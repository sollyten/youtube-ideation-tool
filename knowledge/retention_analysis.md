# Retention Analysis Knowledge Base

This is the PRIMARY reference for the Retention Lab analyzer. It is the enhanced,
structured version of the in-house retention methodology. The analyzer must reason from
this framework first, then may supplement with high-quality external expertise on
YouTube audience retention. It must NOT contradict this framework in favour of generic
advice.

The goal of any retention analysis is to explain, second by second, WHY the curve moves
the way it does, and to convert each movement into a specific, actionable production
note for the next video.

---

## The three lenses

Every retention curve is read through three lenses at once:

1. **Spikes and Dips** — sharp local movements. These reveal moment-to-moment interest:
   what pulled people in, what pushed them away.
2. **Sustained Engagement or Loss** — sections where the curve holds flat or bleeds
   gradually. These reveal emotional, curiosity, and structural health across a stretch.
3. **Overall Chart Behaviour (Audience Journey)** — the shape of the whole curve. This
   reveals total reach quality and whether the video satisfied the audience it drew.

---

## Lens 1: Spikes and Dips

**Spikes** are a strong signal of audience interest: a highlight moment, a timestamp
people jump to, a chapter worth rewatching.

- **Single-tick uptick (unavoidable):** a lone one-frame spike is usually the YouTube
  ad marker, not a content signal. No action needed.
- **Repeated spikes with weak troughs (common on education/advice content):** many
  spikes with low-value stretches between them means viewers are skipping to the good
  parts. The fix is not more spikes, it is raising the value of the material BETWEEN
  them so the whole video is worth watching straight through. Aim for a continuous flow
  of valuable information between spikes.

**Dips** reveal what the audience did not want: a sponsor read, a gross or off-putting
element, a boring segment. Always watch for a lasting loss of engagement that begins
right after a dip or spike, since that is where a moment becomes a trend.

- **Brand-deal dip:** integrate the sponsor so it does not stall momentum. Avoid a
  negative undertone, intertwine the read with the content so progression does not
  stall, and if useful, share the retention chart with the brand to justify a lighter
  touch. Avoid explicit phrases like "sign up" or "sponsored by"; when negotiating the
  deal, ask whether those words can be dropped so the read feels as natural as possible.
- **Exposition dip (too much information at once):** caused by cognitive overload.
  Split information across the video rather than front-loading it. Rule of thumb:
  introduce a piece of information only just before it becomes relevant. If a friend
  appears five minutes in, do not name them at the very start (unless the person is a
  big enough draw to be worth teasing). Deliver unavoidable low-interest material
  (content the viewer did not come for) in short five-second batches.

## Lens 2: Sustained Engagement or Loss

**Sustained engagement** means something is working: a conflict was introduced at an
ignition point, or the video is touching a genuinely relevant aspect of what the viewer
came for (for example, showing the actual process of creating something).

Diagnostic questions for any sustained stretch:
- What were you doing during the drop BEFORE the sustained section began?
- What were you doing DURING the sustained section?
- What were you doing AFTER, when it dropped off?
If it is not clear why a sustained section ends, the most likely cause is simple
boredom.

- **Tension (unresolved conflict):** this is your most valuable pattern. Stretch these
  sections for more watch time, hyper-analyze them to understand exactly what holds the
  audience, and deliberately do more of them in future videos. Keep the conflict open
  and keep introducing interesting, meaningful developments.
- **Second-half flat section:** a flat stretch late in the video teaches little on its
  own. Apply the rules learned elsewhere in the analysis. There is also more room to
  break simple rules briefly here, because the audience still wants to enjoy the ride.

**Sustained loss** is a gradual bleed over a longer stretch: the section lacks something
to engage with, progression is too slow, or conflict is missing (for example, merely
explaining what is going to happen next instead of showing it).

Five points to interrogate on any sustained loss:
- What happened before it?
- Why did it start?
- What happens during it?
- Why did we start, and then stop, losing people?
- Why did engagement not recover afterward?

- **Segmentation loss (a new segment begins):** reduce the build-up into the new
  segment, check whether the PREVIOUS segment resolved too completely (leaving nothing
  to pull the viewer forward), and introduce a new intriguing element quickly. Pace the
  opening segments deliberately: make the first three shorter and faster, then lengthen.
  A working template: Segment 1 slower (about 1 minute), Segment 2 very fast (30 to 45
  seconds), Segment 3 slower again (about 1 minute).
- **Lack of meaningful progression (no sense of moving toward something):** introduce
  milestones, remind the viewer of the goal, and establish visible positive progression
  toward it. Positive progression (building toward something) is good for retention.
  Negative progression (visibly moving toward a goal by removing or dismantling, such as
  clearing a room during a renovation) also works when it is clearly purposeful.

## Lens 3: Overall Chart Behaviour (Audience Journey)

First classify the curve as one of two chart types, because they demand opposite fixes:

- **Static chart:** looks high and flat, which is tempting, but usually signals a
  ceiling on appeal (it is only reaching a narrow, already-loyal audience). Example
  scale: around 150,000 views. Fix: widen the appeal.
- **Dynamic chart:** looks worse at first glance, but signals you are reaching well
  beyond your typical audience, which produces the clearest, most actionable learnings.
  Example scale: around 10,000,000 views. Fix: address the specific issues it exposes.

Then match the curve shape to a named pattern:

- **Gradual boredom (steady downward slope):** introduce more captivating moments,
  create more variety in delivery, add unexpected elements, and impose a clearer
  structure.
- **Interest focused (wavy shape):** the peaks are fine; the problem is the material
  between them. Improve the in-between content and get sharper on what the audience
  actually wants to learn.
- **Core audience only (high and flat):** clean and high, but only because it is serving
  the core audience. Increase the appeal so the content reaches a wider audience.
- **Split experience (mixed signals):** a mismatch in content selection. You cannot
  overdeliver and then follow it with basic filler. Often caused by an unsatisfying
  storyline that never emotionally gratified the viewer, either resolving too early or
  delivering what people came for and then changing too drastically. Make the video feel
  like one coherent whole.
- **Exceptional (rises and holds unusually high):** typically driven by search traffic
  and by viewers actively following along while doing a task. Study what made it work
  and whether it is repeatable.

## Why a retention change might happen (elements to inspect at every notable point)

When the curve moves, check which of these plausibly caused it: scenery changes,
visualization, storytelling structure, music volume, sentence structure, sound effects
and depth, jokes, transition techniques, personality of the character, shot
progression, wide appeal, kind of information, delivery versus expected value, pacing
(the flow of information), side characters, and repetition or implied repetition.

---

## How to record findings (note-taking standard)

For every notable movement in the curve, log:
- the beginning and end timestamp of the section,
- the note type: Correctness, Mistake, Opportunity, or Other,
- the diagnosis and the specific production fix.
Keep every completed analysis on file as reference, so patterns across videos compound.
